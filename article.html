The $6.34B Binance Hack: How EIP-7702 and a Fake Interview Bypassed the Signing Layer

Published: November 5, 2026 • 8 min read
Advertisement

In the early hours of October 29, 2026, Binance suffered the largest security breach in crypto history. Over $6.3 billion was drained from its hot wallets. But here is the uncomfortable truth: No private key was stolen. The blockchain didn't break. The cryptography didn't fail. The attackers simply found a way to bypass the layer that tells the keys what to sign.

The TL;DR

  • The Entry Point: Social engineering via fake job interviews and a tampered Homebrew package.
  • The Mechanism: Ethereum's EIP-7702 was abused to delegate wallet authority to a malicious smart contract.
  • The Bypass: Attackers obtained 50 pre-signed EIP-7702 authorizations from Binance's internal signing service.
  • The Result: A $6.34B heist across multiple chains, without a single leaked key.

Phase 1: The Setup (June – September 2026)

The attack was not a smash-and-grab. It was a multi-stage campaign executed over months. According to Binance's official incident report and Halborn's technical breakdown, the intrusion chain began with a single engineer:

  • June 2026: A Binance Wallet DevOps engineer downloaded a tampered version of Homebrew while searching for an AI coding tool. Binance detected the anomaly and replaced the device, but the attackers had already mapped internal roles and colleagues.
  • September 2026: Attackers targeted another engineer through a legitimate job platform. Posing as recruiters, they conducted fake interviews and sent a "coding assignment" that contained malware. This granted them persistent access to the internal network.

By October, the attackers had reached what they really wanted: the internal wallet signing service.

Phase 2: The Exploit (October 29, 2026)

The core of the attack relied on a legitimate Ethereum feature: EIP-7702. Introduced in 2025, EIP-7702 allows a regular Externally Owned Account (EOA) to delegate its execution logic to a smart contract. It was designed to improve UX (e.g., allowing wallets to batch transactions or sponsor gas). But it also creates a powerful attack vector if an attacker can obtain signed authorizations.

How It Unfolded:

  1. Preparation: The attacker deployed a malicious "sweeper" contract designed to transfer assets from any wallet that delegates to it.
  2. The 50 Signatures: Over ~4.5 hours, the attacker sent a transaction containing 50 EIP-7702 authorizations to the Binance signing service. These authorizations corresponded to a range of nonces (from 18,914,775 to 18,914,824). The attacker was betting that one would match the wallet's current nonce.
  3. The Delegation: One of the 50 signatures was accepted. This effectively "upgraded" the Binance hot wallet to include the attacker's sweeper contract logic. The wallet was now a puppet.
  4. The Drain: Four minutes later, the attacker called the sweeper function. Funds began flowing out. In a single transaction, 167,464 ETH and over $1.6B in USDT were moved. Within 20 minutes, the drain spread across ~10 blockchains.

Crucial distinction: EIP-7702 itself was not "hacked." The attacker simply asked the signing service to sign authorization messages, and the service complied. The signing layer did not distinguish between a standard withdrawal and a delegation authorization.

Why It Worked (and Why It's Terrifying)

For years, the crypto industry focused on protecting private keys—using MPC, HSMs, and multisig setups. But this attack proves that the signing layer is equally critical.

As Halborn noted, the attacker didn't forge withdrawal orders; they reached the component that produces signatures and "asked it directly." The policy controls that Binance applied to withdrawals lived in a layer the attacker walked around. The keys were secure; the system telling the keys what to sign was not.

Furthermore, because Binance 14 used MPC (Multi-Party Computation), revoking the delegation was slow. There was no single private key to sign a revocation. The team had to develop a "specialized procedure" to revoke the on-chain delegation, which took nearly four hours.

The Takeaway for Builders and Users

  • For Custody Architects: If you run an MPC hot wallet on any EVM chain, you must treat signing authorization requests with the same scrutiny as withdrawal requests. A signer should parse what it is signing, not just blindly authorize.
  • For Engineers: Social engineering is still the most effective entry point. Fake job interviews and poisoned developer tools (like Homebrew) are actively being used by APT groups (attributed to North Korea's TraderTraitor).
  • For Users: Your funds on a centralized exchange are only as safe as the exchange's internal infrastructure. Self-custody (with hardware wallets) remains the only way to truly own your keys.
